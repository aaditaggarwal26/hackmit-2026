A satellite takes far more pictures than it can ever send home.

A spacecraft can only downlink while it is passing over a ground station. Most of what it captured in between is cloud, haze, or a scene that has not changed since yesterday, and it has no way of knowing which is which. So the window gets spent on whatever happened to be next in the buffer. Orbit scores every frame on the satellite that took it, and spends the window best first.

Run one pass yourself See the measured result

The camera is not the constraint. The window is.

Downlink happens only during a pass over a ground station, and a satellite cannot widen that window: it is a matter of line of sight and licensed spectrum, not of engineering effort. The buffer fills faster than the link can drain it, so something has to decide what leaves first. Today, nothing does.

Every frame is the same size

128 × 128 pixels, 8-bit grayscale, one byte per pixel. It leaves the board as 19 chunks of 900 raw bytes, repeated over three whole passes, because the shared bus loses datagrams.

16,384 B

The modelled window carries 60

120 seconds at 65,536 bits per second is 983,040 bytes, which is sixty frames. Both policies are handed that same budget, and the comparison is what each one chose to spend it on. The window is modelled by byte accounting, not radiated: pixels never cross a radio.

Modelled

29% of the corpus is cloudy

56 of the 194 committed MODIS frames are classified cloudy by the scoring kernel's own threshold. Those are the frames a first-in-first-out satellite spends its window on roughly a third of the time, and they are the ones that arrive worthless.

56 / 194

Losing a frame is not the failure mode

Cloud lowers a frame's score. It does not block the radio. What gates transmission is line of sight, and those two things stay separate everywhere in this system, because conflating them is how a demo gets taken apart.

By design

Score it where the picture was taken.

Three integer measurements over the frame's own pixels, one streaming pass, no multiplication in the inner loop and no neural network. It is deliberately simple: it has to run on a microcontroller, and it has to give the same answer every time.

What it measures

Every term is a count of pixels, weighted equally at 21,845 of 65,535.

A pixel counts as cloud above 200, and as changed when it differs from its scene's reference frame by more than 16. Sharpness is a Sobel sum shifted right by 5, a value tuned once against the corpus and then frozen. Scores are unsigned 16-bit on the wire and shown here out of 100.

Reorder, do not discard

The difference that makes this defensible.

ESA's Φ-sat-1 showed in 2020 that a satellite can filter its own imagery onboard, using an AI accelerator to throw cloudy frames away before they were ever sent. Orbit only changes the order.

If the score is wrong about a frame, that frame is still on the satellite, just further back in the queue. Nothing is destroyed on the strength of a number computed by a microcontroller. It is a better failure mode for a hackathon, and a better one for a real mission.

One window, several satellites, one decision at a time.

Scoring onboard a single satellite has flown before. What this project is actually about is the next problem: several satellites sharing one ground station. Each bids its best frame, the ground grants one slot to one satellite, that satellite transmits to completion, and the arbitration runs again.

Solid lines are the control bus. The dashed line is telemetry only: the laptop is never in the control path, so closing it does not stop the run. Satellites are identified by numbered lane rather than by colour, so the display survives a bad projector.

How one slot is decided

The frame's scoreWhat the kernel measured, out of 65,535. score
+ How long that frame has waitedStops a good frame being stranded behind newer arrivals. 0.5 / s
+ How long that satellite has waitedStops a slightly lower-scoring satellite being locked out, which frame aging alone can never fix. 0.3 / s

Evaluated per slot, on each satellite's top item only. No round robin, no time slicing, no preemption: a satellite that keeps winning genuinely holds the best frames. Ties go to the lowest hostname, so a run replays identically. When the two aging terms rather than the raw score decide a slot, the ground labels that grant starvation_forced in the event stream, which is how fairness is audited after the fact rather than asserted.

Usable means a cloud fraction of at most 0.35, computed from the delivered frame and never from the score that ranked it, because ranking by a number and then reporting that same number is circular.

The scoring kernel, timed

11,392 ± 27 frames per second on one pinned Cortex-X925 core, and 7,811 ± 9 on the GB10 GPU, over the same 194 MODIS frames. This is a single-frame-per-call microbenchmark with a host synchronisation per frame, so it is a latency figure, not a throughput contest, and Orbit makes no claim about beating a datacentre GPU.

GX10

1.63 J per 1,000 frames

GPU-die only, from the NVML energy counter. CPU and whole-board power are not instrumented on that machine and are reported as unavailable rather than estimated. Die figures and board figures are never compared as though they were the same quantity.

GPU die

Redundancy is what makes the bus usable

Measured on the demo network by counting gaps in each node's own sequence numbers: a single copy of every datagram loses 35.7%. Three copies four milliseconds apart lose 13.3%. Four copies twenty milliseconds apart lose 3.6%, which is what the firmware ships.

On the bench

The same scoring function, running on whatever your laptop camera is pointed at. It shows the cloud mask, the edge map and the change mask that produced the number, and whether the priority queue chose to keep the frame or evict it.

What it proves

Onboard scoring and queue admission, live, on an image nobody prepared in advance. A photo comes in, the kernel scores it on cloud, detail and change, and the eight-slot queue decides whether it is worth keeping.

What is measured, what is modelled, and what is scaled down.

The project's first principle is that it never invents a number. Anything that has not been measured says so, with the reason. This is the section to read before asking a hard question, because the hard question has probably already been answered here.

Measured

The scoring kernel's speed and GPU-die energy, on the GX10, over the committed corpus. The multicast loss rates, on the demo network. The board's flash and PSRAM, read off the chip rather than off the datasheet. 353 tests, of which two need that machine's Linux scheduler calls and do not run anywhere else.

Modelled

The contact window. Pixels never cross a radio: the link carries frame rows in and scores, grants and accounting out, and the window's capacity is enforced by byte accounting. The demo window is deliberately scaled down to three frames per pass, and it is labelled as scaled everywhere it appears.

Not measured

CPU and whole-board power on the GX10, which needs an inline USB-C power meter nobody had. Per-frame energy on the satellite itself, because no board is instrumented. These read unavailable, with the reason, and that is the correct behaviour rather than a gap.

Open

Contact-window duration in a real mission is not something this project has established, so no figure for it appears anywhere. The same goes for camera hardware and for whether a flight system would capture live or replay.

Bit-exact

The firmware's C kernel and the Python reference model are held to the same answer, pixel for pixel, by a parity checker in the test suite. The number a board reports and the number the simulator reports are the same number, which is what lets a board drop off the bus mid-demo without invalidating the run.

What a real mission would need next.

Not a roadmap of features. These are the four things that stand between a weekend demonstration of the idea and something that would survive a power and timing budget.

Scoring in the camera's data path

A general-purpose microcontroller keeps up with a demo workload. A live sensor at flight data rates cannot wait on software sharing a CPU, so the same integer pass belongs in an FPGA sitting directly in the data path. It is the same arithmetic; what changes is where it runs.

Hardware

Energy measured on the satellite

The claim that scoring costs less than transmitting is the economic argument for the whole idea, and right now this project cannot measure the satellite side of it. An instrumented board turns the strongest assumption into a number.

Instrumentation

A scoring function with an error bar

Brightness variance is a proxy for usefulness, chosen for being fast and predictable. The honest next step is to measure how often it disagrees with a human about which frame was worth sending, and publish that disagreement rate rather than assert the proxy is good.

Validation

More satellites than slots

Arbitration is the part nobody has flown, and three nodes is the smallest number that makes it interesting. The aging terms are what should hold at twelve; that is a claim this project has not yet tested and would want to before making it.

Built with.

What each sponsor's hardware or platform actually did in this build, rather than a logo wall.

EspressifESP32-S3

The satellites themselves. Each board runs the scoring kernel in C, holds its own eight-slot priority queue in a 128 KB PSRAM pool allocated once at boot, and bids for transmission slots over UDP multicast. The C kernel is held bit-exact against the Python reference model, so a board and a simulated satellite are interchangeable mid-run. Frames live in on-board flash; nothing is fetched at demo time.

ASUSGX10 · display

The GX10 is where every performance number in this project was measured. The same integer kernel a satellite runs was timed on its CPU and its GB10 GPU over the same 194 MODIS frames: 11,392 ± 27 frames per second on one pinned Cortex-X925 core, 7,811 ± 9 on the GPU, and 1.63 J per thousand frames from the GPU-die energy counter. A microcontroller can tell you the idea works. This is the machine that answers the question a microcontroller cannot — what the same arithmetic costs when the frame rate is real, which is the whole of the scaling argument. Its display drove the live demo.

VoloridgeThe dataset

The imagery is NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services: real MODIS Terra true-colour tiles, not synthetic frames and not a handful picked to flatter the result. 194 of them are committed to the repository across 14 scenes and 16 dates, each carrying its request URL, its date, its scene and a sha256, with NASA's usage statement stored verbatim beside them. The corpus is not decoration: a sweep of the scoring kernel across all 194 frames is what set the demo's pacing, and the rule that pacing had to clear was fixed before the run, not after it. The same frames are on the boards, in the simulator and in the benchmark, so every number on this page traces back to the same pixels.
